The water pushed heavily against his shins, flowing down from the hills beyond. It was cold.

The brush behind him rustled. He shifted his head to look where the noise was coming from. Pushing through it from an off-trail, there was a woman. She had salt-peppered brown hair and was dressed in very old and worn cotton from head to toe. On her back, she carried a covered basket. She looked up and paused for a moment, locking her eyes in contact with Diogo. "You look like you could use some help," she said, her still face cracking into a small smirk. 

Diogo grunted, turning back to the streaming river. "Don't worry bout me, sister. I've survived like this for years." He crouched back down, feeling the numbness of his toes beginning to set in through his thick callouses. 

Just barely above the trickling and bubbling river, Diogo could hear the woman curse as she was rummaging something out of her basket, before letting out a sigh. Diogo turned as he heard the hurried weight of her steps as she trodded to the edge of the stream where Diogo was standing.

The woman had stuck her arm out towards him, holding a net. "If you wanna fish, you can use my net while I'm pickin." Her green eyes stared directly at Diogo's. 

He shifted backwards in his stance, but lost his footing on the muddy bank. His knee, on the same leg as his slipped foot, stung with great pain as it crashed onto a rock. He grunted through clenched teeth and looked back up to the woman. She kept eye contact with her green eyes. Waiting.

Diogo leaned forward and raised his leg, now caked in mud and rocks, and pushed himself up from it. Now standing, he looked to the net. It was frayed but sturdy. He looked back to the woman, below her eye line, then back to the river. "No thanks," he grunted. "I've been doing this for long enough."

"Suit yourself then," she responded. Diogo could hear her shuffling away, alongside a small thud beside him. He briefly glanced, spotting the net placed atop her basket about 10 feet away. The woman began to crouch down by a brush not too far beyond. Diogo grunted, and rolled his eyes back to the stream. 

"The sounds of the river's nature is quite nice, don't you think stranger?" she asked.

The air seemed to be filled even greater with the sounds of the river bank. The stream trickling down the rocky bed. The wind flowing through the leaves of trees and bushes. The occasional chirping of birds joined in melody. 

"It is one of the Oakfather's blessings on this land," Diogo responded. 

"You're not one for conversation, are you stranger?" she asked.

Diogo grunted. 

The woman chuckled. "That response reminds of my son you know."

Diogo grunted again. He could hear the woman continue to gather, but he kept his eyes stayed fixed upon the deep blue water flowing down from upstream. His feet were steady but his knees were already getting sore.

The woman returned before Diogo, a tray of herbs and berries in her hands. Her open green eyes stared into his. "My son was in the war," she spoke. It was firm and directed. "He said it was his duty to help this nation. But when he came back... Well, I just don't understand why he couldn't seem to accept the same help he gave his soul to provide." 

Diogo scowled and shot his eyes from the river to the woman. She had bit her lip and held her eyes wide open to him. She was unflinching. "I ain't your son ma'am," he barked, shifting his eyes back to the river. His heart began to race, chest tightening and migraine spiking. "And I don't need your net. Fuck off."

"Alright stranger. Nature's blessings to ya." Out of the corner of his eye, he could see her turn around. She stacked the net and tray into her basket, strapping them onto her back, and continued down the path she came from.

Diogo sighed, muttering unintelligibly beneath his breath. He focused back to the river.

Minutes passed and Diogo spotted a shadow. He traced it for a few seconds, seeing it follow the current until it became clearer. A school of fish. They were rushing down the water. 

Diogo dropped his knees further, leaning in with his hands hovering above the water. 

Ten feet away. He clenched his hands, and opened them back up. 

Five feet. He took in a breath. 

One foot. 

He crashed his hands into the water, aiming for the two large spots. He closed them the moment he felt a force rub against his palms. He could not feel what he had grabbed, as the cold water and thick skin had numbed his hands. He raised his hands up and slowly unfurled his fingers. Two minnows, barely the size of a carrot.

His stomach rumbled.
