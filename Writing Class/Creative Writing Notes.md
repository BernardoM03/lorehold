Violence is the easy answer because it makes the scene's meaning obvious. A man dies, so we know it mattered.

So the first move when you stall is not to search harder for details. It's to ask what he wants in this room in the next thirty seconds. Not his life's ambition. The immediate one

**One. What does he want in the next minute, and what is stopping him?** Answer in a short sentence. If your answer is abstract, you'll stall again. "Respect" produces nothing. "To get out of the room without being asked a second time" produces a door, a distance, a man between him and it.

**Two. What is he doing with his hands?** This is the cheapest reliable source of detail in prose and it's underused. A man in a bar wanting to leave does something with a cup, a strap, a coin, the edge of a table. You already know this instinctively. The straps on the backpack in scene three are pure hands, and they're the most alive thing in that scene.

**Three. What does he look at that he shouldn't, and what does he avoid looking at?** Attention is characterized by what it refuses as much as what it lands on. Diogo not looking at Alric is as informative as Diogo looking at Alric.

**Four. What would he notice that nobody else in the room would?** This is the one that produces character rather than scenery. Diogo is a farmer's son. He knows soil, weather, animals, stores, what a winter costs. Put him in a monastery and he should be reading the room like a man who has run a farmstead: how much food is here, who's been fed, is the roof sound, is that man's robe worth more than the plough. That's Mantel's whole method with Cromwell and cloth. His trade is his eye.
